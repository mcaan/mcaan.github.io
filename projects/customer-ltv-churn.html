Customer LTV & Churn Predictions

Using retail transactions to identify valuable customers, estimate three-month purchasing outcomes, and design a retention decision that could be tested in an experiment.

UCI Online RetailPython · pandas · scikit-learnRFM · Forecasting · Classification

View code and full README on GitHub ↗

The question

At a fixed decision date, which repeat customers appear most valuable, who is unlikely to purchase in the next three months, and when might outreach be worth its cost?

The analysis uses 1,926 eligible customers and information available before September 1, 2011. Purchases through November provide the outcome window. “Churn” here means no purchase in that window, not permanent customer loss.

The approach

01

Clean transactions and reconcile cancellations by what was knowable at the decision date; assign a fixed 60/20/20 train, validation, and test split.

02

Describe historic value with RFM segments and compare three-month revenue estimates. Model the probability of no future purchase using only pre-decision features.

03

Choose an illustrative outreach rule on validation, test sensitivity to assumed prices and margin, then evaluate the locked rule once on the held-out test set.

What the models found

Historic value was concentrated: median calibration revenue was about £539, while the mean was about £1,508. For individual three-month revenue, simple historic-rate extrapolation beat the fitted gradient boosting regression on validation mean absolute error (£900.71 vs £996.92).

For inactivity prediction, gradient boosting was modestly stronger than logistic regression on validation discrimination and probability quality. The chart shows the two ranking metrics; the Brier scores were 0.1755 and 0.1811, respectively.

From model to decision

Under illustrative segment-specific outreach prices and a 25% assumed margin, the lowest modeled validation loss came from gradient boosting at a churn-probability threshold of 0.09. The selected rule was locked before test evaluation.

A prespecified 0.06 alternative did better on the held-out test set, and only three of nine price and margin scenarios favored the 0.09 rule. The result shows why a threshold should be treated as a decision under assumptions rather than as a property of the model.

What comes next

The proposed next step is to randomize newly eligible, flagged customers within segment to outreach or no contact, then measure incremental contribution net of actual outreach costs.

The source data record no outreach treatment or response. The modeled loss figures are scenario estimates, not measured savings; three months of observed revenue are not full lifetime value.
